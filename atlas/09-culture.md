# Pi in culture

Pi is the only number with its own holiday, and it did not stop there. It has a cologne, at least one film, a Kate Bush song, and a small body of poetry written so that the word lengths spell out its digits. This chapter is about pi outside the textbook: how March 14 became a party recognized by the United Nations, why people memorize thousands of digits for sport, how pi turns up in movies and novels and one very confused piece of 1897 legislation, and what it says about us that a ratio about circles gets cake.

## Pi Day

Pi Day is March 14, and the date is a pun you read straight off the number. Pi begins 3.14, and in American month/day notation March 14 is written 3/14. The holiday was invented in 1988 by Larry Shaw, a physicist at the Exploratorium, the science museum in San Francisco. He and the staff marked it by parading around the museum and eating fruit pie, the edible pun sitting right next to the mathematical one.

The starting time was chosen with the same care. The first celebration kicked off at 1:59 in the afternoon, because the digits immediately after 3.14 are 1, 5, 9. Read the date and the clock together and you get 3.14159, pi to five decimals. Shaw added a circular brass plaque he called the Pi Shrine, a parade set to Pomp and Circumstance, and a round of Happy Birthday to Albert Einstein, who really was born on March 14. He led the parade himself every year until he died in 2017.

The party grew up and went official. On March 12, 2009 the United States House of Representatives passed a resolution (H.Res. 224) supporting the designation of Pi Day and encouraging schools to use it to get students interested in mathematics. Ten years later pi went global: in November 2019 the 40th General Conference of UNESCO proclaimed March 14 the International Day of Mathematics. A staff gathering at one museum had become a date on the calendar of a United Nations agency.

## Pi Approximation Day

Pi has a second feast day for the fraction people actually used before decimals were common. Pi Approximation Day is July 22, because in day/month order that is 22/7, and $22/7 = 3.142857\ldots$ is the classic ancient estimate. It is also a genuinely good one: $22/7$ lands closer to pi than the rounded 3.14 does. So the calendar honors pi twice, once for the opening of its decimal ($3/14$) and once for its friendliest fraction ($22/7$).

## Piphilology: writing that counts

Piphilology is the hobby of building mnemonics for the digits of pi. The rule is simple and strict: each digit becomes a word, and the number of letters in the word is the digit. The best known English example, often credited to the physicist James Jeans, is a single sentence:

> How I need a drink, alcoholic of course, after the heavy lectures involving quantum mechanics.

Count the letters. How (3), I (1), need (4), a (1), drink (5), alcoholic (9), of (2), course (6), after (5), the (3), heavy (5), lectures (8), involving (9), quantum (7), mechanics (9). Read the counts in order and you have $3.14159265358979$, the first fifteen digits.

The game scales as far as anyone's patience. Mike Keith's 1996 story "Cadaeic Cadenza" is written entirely under this constraint, its word lengths tracking the first 3835 digits of pi in order, and it manages to parody famous poems along the way (it opens by rewriting Poe's "The Raven"). A zero is handled by a ten-letter word, and longer digit strings force ever more inventive vocabulary. This is constrained writing, a small literary genre where an artificial rule does the steering, and the dialect Keith writes in even has a nickname, "Pilish." He later stretched it into a whole book.

Plain memorization is a sport of its own. People recite thousands of digits from memory before witnesses, and the record chase is worth its own telling, in [Records](06-records.md).

## Pi on screen and page

For an irrational number, pi has an unusual number of credits.

Carl Sagan's 1985 novel "Contact" closes on a mathematical grace note. A character learns that if you compute pi far enough, in the right number base, the endless random-looking digits eventually snap into order: a block of ones and zeros that, laid out as a grid, draws a circle. The suggestion is that a message was tucked inside the universe's most famous constant. It is fiction (no such pattern has been found, and pi is widely believed to be normal, its digits patternless), but it is a lovely thought about a number that already feels like it contains everything.

Darren Aronofsky borrowed the symbol for the title of his first film, "Pi" (1998), a black-and-white thriller about a number theorist convinced that one numerical pattern underlies the stock market, nature, and the mind, and who is hunted for it. The number barely appears; pi stands in for the obsessive search for hidden order, which is the whole mood of the film.

Kate Bush set pi to music. Her song "Pi," on the 2005 album "Aerial," is sung in the voice of a man in love with the number, and she does the improbable thing of singing the actual digits. Listeners quickly noticed she does not sing them all: she recites dozens of decimal places, drops a run of them, and gets a couple wrong. Whether that was an error or a choice is still cheerfully argued by fans who have checked her against the true expansion, which is an extremely on-brand thing to argue about.

Then the coincidences and cameos. Yann Martel's "Life of Pi" (2001) has nothing to do with the number: the narrator is named Piscine Molitor Patel and shortens it to "Pi" so classmates will stop mocking "Piscine." The overlap is a pun and nothing more. On television, The Simpsons has told pi jokes for decades, most quotably when Professor Frink silences a hall of scientists by shouting that pi is exactly three. And in 1998 the fashion house Givenchy launched a men's cologne called Pi, sold on the number's air of mystery. A ratio about circles turned into a bottle on a shelf.

## The Indiana Pi Bill

In 1897 the Indiana General Assembly came within one legislative chamber of writing a false value of pi into state law. A physician and amateur mathematician named Edward J. Goodwin believed he had squared the circle, the ancient construction proven impossible, and offered his method to the state as House Bill 246, royalty free, as a gift. The bill's prose is tangled and never states pi outright, but its geometry pins a value down. One clause fixes "the ratio of the diameter and circumference" at "five-fourths to four," which rearranges to a circumference-to-diameter ratio of $16/5 = 3.2$. That is pi wrong in the second decimal place. Compute areas by that rule and every circle comes out roughly two percent too large. Worse, other sentences in the bill imply still different values, so it is not even wrong in a single consistent way.

The bill sailed through the Indiana House without a dissenting vote, because the representatives took it for a harmless piece of educational novelty rather than a mathematical claim. It died in the Senate purely by luck. C. A. Waldo, a mathematics professor from Purdue, happened to be at the statehouse on unrelated business, saw what was about to happen, and briefed the senators well enough that they postponed the bill indefinitely. The real defect was never the arithmetic but the premise. Squaring the circle with straightedge and compass had already been proven impossible in 1882, when Lindemann showed pi is transcendental (see [What is pi](01-what-is-pi.md)). Goodwin was legislating an answer to a question that provably has none. Pi does not take amendments.

## Why a number gets a holiday

Most numbers are tools you use and forget. Pi is different because it feels discovered rather than invented. It was here before us, it turns up uninvited in physics and probability where no circle is in sight, and its digits run on forever without repeating, which reads like a small infinity you can hold in one hand. That combination, utterly familiar (it is just circles) and utterly endless (nobody will ever write it all), is the thing people are really celebrating. The pie and the puns are the excuse. The holiday is for the rare pleasure of a fact that is at once completely settled and completely inexhaustible, and for the very human habit of throwing a party for something true.
